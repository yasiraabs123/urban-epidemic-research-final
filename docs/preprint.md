## Abstract

Traditional compartmental epidemic models assume that the underlying contact network is static, an assumption that empirical mobility data from the COVID-19 pandemic has repeatedly contradicted. We present a comparative study of **static** and **dynamic** contact-network SEIR models across three urban spatial regimes — **Dense, Polycentric, and Sparse** — generated by a Stochastic Block Model (SBM) with a Fermi–Dirac distance kernel. The dynamic model layers two temporal mechanisms on top of the SBM substrate: a **weekly contact rhythm** (Weekend = 0.6 × Weekday) and **prevalence-threshold behavioural adaptation** (0 %, 30 %, 50 %, 70 % contact reduction at increasing infection levels). Across 90 stochastic simulations (30 per network), dynamic models predict peak-day delays of 7–12 days and attack-rate reductions of 6–14 percentage points relative to static baselines, with the largest effect in Dense topologies (paired *t* = 4.82, *p* = 0.041; Cohen's *d* = 1.96, large effect). The dynamic model validates against St. Petersburg COVID-19 monitoring data from Winter 2022 with **R² = 0.915**, **RMSE = 0.087**, and **Pearson r = 0.957**, compared with R² = 0.62 for the static baseline. Behavioural analysis reveals diminishing returns: a 50 % contact reduction captures approximately 72 % of the benefit of a 70 % reduction at half the social cost. Optimal intervention timing is shown to be topology-dependent — Dense cities require action by day 18–20, Polycentric by day 28, and Sparse by day 42. Topology-tailored strategies are approximately twice as effective as uniform interventions. The framework provides a lightweight, reproducible alternative to single-city digital-twin models and motivates a shift from one-size-fits-all to topology-aware public-health policy.

**Keywords —** Epidemic modelling, SEIR, contact networks, Stochastic Block Model, behavioural adaptation, COVID-19, network epidemiology, intervention timing, urban morphology, St. Petersburg.

## I. Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated, at planetary scale, that disease transmission is a **socio-technical phenomenon** rather than a purely biological one. Human contact patterns vary by day of the week, by perceived risk, and by policy regime. Yet the most widely used class of epidemic models — compartmental SIR / SEIR frameworks on static contact networks — assumes that the network is fixed throughout the outbreak [1], [3]. This produces two systematic distortions of practical importance:

1. **Peak severity is overestimated**, because the model never allows contact rates to fall in response to rising prevalence.
2. **Peak timing is underestimated**, because no behavioural delay is built in.

Both distortions are policy-relevant: a model that predicts a peak that is too early and too high will recommend interventions that are mis-timed, often deployed when they can no longer "bend the curve" but only "flatten the tail."

A growing body of literature has introduced **dynamic** elements into epidemic networks — activity-driven models [9], [10], behaviour-coupled models [7], [8], and mobility-driven digital twins [5], [11], [12]. However, these approaches typically focus on one ingredient at a time (rhythm *or* feedback), one city at a time, and often at a computational cost that precludes systematic comparison across **urban topology classes**.

In this paper we ask a deceptively simple question:

> *How do dynamic contact patterns — combining weekly rhythms and threshold-based behavioural adaptation — change predicted outbreak dynamics, and how does the magnitude of that effect depend on the city's spatial topology?*

We answer it by constructing three synthetic urban contact networks (Dense, Polycentric, Sparse) via a Stochastic Block Model, running dynamic and static SEIR simulations on each, and validating the dynamic model against real St. Petersburg COVID-19 data from Winter 2022.

**Contributions of this work:**

1. The **first systematic static-vs-dynamic comparison** across three urban spatial regimes under matched disease parameters.
2. A **unified dynamic SEIR framework** that integrates weekly contact rhythms *and* prevalence-threshold behavioural adaptation on the same SBM substrate.
3. **Out-of-sample validation** against St. Petersburg COVID-19 monitoring data (R² = 0.915) using literature-derived parameters.
4. **Topology-specific intervention timing guidelines** that empirically demonstrate a 2× effectiveness gain over one-size-fits-all policy.
5. A fully reproducible, **open-source code release** with all figures regenerated end-to-end from a modular Python codebase.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II surveys related work. Section III presents the methodology — network generation, dynamic SEIR specification, and statistical evaluation. Section IV describes the implementation. Section V reports results across the six analytical tasks. Section VI discusses implications and limitations. Section VII concludes and outlines future work.

## II. Related Work

A full Related Work review is provided in the companion document `docs/related_work.md`. A condensed summary follows.

**Static network epidemiology** (Pastor-Satorras & Vespignani [1]; Newman [2]; Keeling & Eames [3]) established that network topology — degree distribution, clustering, assortativity — governs outbreak shape. These models assume time-invariant networks and cannot represent behavioural change.

**Synthetic populations and digital twins** (Eubank et al., EpiSimS [6]; Bilal et al., CitySEIRCast [5]; Rineer et al. [4]) achieve high realism for **one** locale but are computationally heavy and do not enable cross-topology comparison.

**Dynamic and behaviour-coupled models** (Funk et al. [7], [8]; Perra et al. [9]; Karsai et al. [10]; Aleta et al. [11]; Chang et al. [12]) introduce time-varying contact patterns or behavioural feedback, but generally implement *one* dynamic mechanism on *one* topology.

The **gap** the present work fills is the simultaneous combination of (a) three urban topology classes, (b) weekly rhythms, (c) prevalence-threshold behavioural feedback, and (d) real-data validation in a single, lightweight, reproducible framework.

## III. Methodology

### A. Network Generation

Each city is represented as an undirected graph *G = (V, E)* with **|V| = 1,000** nodes. Nodes are partitioned into three blocks reflecting heterogeneous social roles:

| Block        | Size | Description                                                |
| ------------ | ---- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Social       | 435  | High intra-block contact rate (households, friend groups). |
| Hub          | 75   | High inter-block connectivity (transportation, workplaces).|
| Non-social   | 490  | Low contact rate (isolated or remote individuals).         |

Edge probabilities follow a Stochastic Block Model with a Fermi–Dirac distance modulation:

$$
P(i \sim j) = P_{b(i)b(j)} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\frac{d(i,j) - \mu}{T}\right)}
$$

where *b(i)* denotes the block of node *i*, *d(i,j)* is the Euclidean distance between their assigned 2-D positions, *μ* is the spatial-mixing parameter, and *T* is a softness constant.

Three values of *μ* generate the three urban regimes:

| Regime           | *μ* | Avg. degree | Clustering | Interpretation                       |
| ---------------- | --- | ----------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Sparse           |  5  | 16.5        | 0.67       | Suburban / small regional city.      |
| Polycentric      | 10  | 31.4        | 0.42       | Multi-centre metropolitan layout.    |
| Dense            | 15  | 49.1        | 0.31       | Single-core high-mixing megacity.    |

Figure 1 visualises the three networks.

### B. Dynamic SEIR Model

A discrete-time SEIR model is simulated for **T = 120 days** with COVID-19 wild-type parameters [14]:

| Parameter                    | Symbol | Value                |
| ---------------------------- | ------ | -------------------- |
| Transmission rate            | β      | 0.28 / day           |
| Incubation rate              | σ      | 1 / 5.2 / day        |
| Recovery rate                | γ      | 1 / 7.0 / day        |
| Basic reproduction number    | R₀     | 2.5                  |
| Initial infected             | I₀     | 50 nodes (0.1 % of N) |
| Simulation horizon           | T      | 120 days             |

The transition rates per node are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S \to E & : \quad \lambda_i(t) = \beta \cdot c(t) \cdot \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbb{1}[X_j(t) = I] \\
E \to I & : \quad \sigma \\
I \to R & : \quad \gamma
\end{aligned}
$$

where *c(t)* is a time-varying **contact multiplier**:

$$
c(t) = w(t) \cdot a(t)
$$

with *w(t)* the weekly rhythm and *a(t)* the behavioural-adaptation factor.

**Weekly rhythm.**

$$
w(t) = \begin{cases}
1.0 & \text{if } t \bmod 7 \in \{0,\ldots,4\} \quad \text{(weekday)} \\
0.6 & \text{if } t \bmod 7 \in \{5,6\} \quad \text{(weekend)}
\end{cases}
$$

calibrated against Google COVID-19 Community Mobility Reports [15].

**Behavioural adaptation.**

$$
a(t) = \begin{cases}
1.0 & \text{if } I(t)/N < 1\% \\
0.7 & \text{if } 1\% \le I(t)/N < 5\% \\
0.5 & \text{if } 5\% \le I(t)/N < 10\% \\
0.3 & \text{if } I(t)/N \ge 10\%
\end{cases}
$$

calibrated against published mobility-reduction data during the 2020 lockdowns [7], [8], [15].

### C. Static Baseline

The static baseline uses the same network *G* and the same disease parameters, with *c(t) ≡ 1*, removing both the weekly rhythm and behavioural adaptation.

### D. Stochastic Simulation Design

For each of the three networks, **30 independent stochastic simulations** are run with different random seeds, yielding **N = 90 dynamic runs** plus 30 matched static runs per topology. Outcomes recorded per run: outbreak curve *I(t)/N*, peak day *t\**, peak height *I*\*\*/N*, and final attack rate *A = R(T)/N*.

### E. Statistical Evaluation

Static vs. dynamic outcomes are compared with:

* **Paired t-test** across the three city-level mean shifts.
* **Cohen's *d*** for effect-size magnitude.
* **One-way ANOVA** across topologies, *post hoc* Tukey HSD.

### F. Validation Against Real Data

The dynamic-model output for the Dense regime is compared against normalised St. Petersburg COVID-19 monitoring data from the Winter 2022 (Omicron) wave. Goodness-of-fit metrics:

* **Coefficient of determination R²**
* **Root-mean-square error (RMSE)**
* **Mean absolute error (MAE)**
* **Pearson correlation r**

## IV. Implementation

The full pipeline is implemented in **Python 3.10+** with NetworkX, NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib. Source layout:

```
src/
├── network.py        # Stochastic Block Model network generation
├── seir.py           # Discrete-time SEIR with weekly + behavioural dynamics
├── experiments.py    # Experiment driver: baselines + behavior + timing sweeps
├── analysis.py       # Statistical metrics (t-test, ANOVA, Cohen's d, R², RMSE)
└── figures.py        # Publication figure generation

data/                 # Validation reference data
figures/              # Generated PNG figures
results/              # Final deliverables (deck, speaker notes)
docs/                 # This preprint and the Related Work review
```

All modules are runnable from the repository root. Reproduction of every figure and metric in this paper requires only:

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m src.experiments --runs 30 --seed 42
python -m src.figures
python -m src.analysis
```

The total wall-clock cost for the 90 stochastic baseline SEIR simulations (plus behavior and timing scenario sweeps) on a 1,000-node network is on the order of minutes on a standard laptop — substantially lighter than the digital-twin / ABM approaches of [5], [6], [11].

## V. Results and Evaluation

### A. Task 1 — Network Properties

Figure 1 shows the three SBM networks side-by-side. Structural statistics confirm a monotonic gradient: Sparse networks have the lowest average degree (16.5) and highest clustering (0.67), while Dense networks have the highest average degree (49.1) and lowest clustering (0.31). These structural differences drive the downstream outbreak differences in Sections V-B to V-E.

### B. Task 3 — Static vs. Dynamic Outbreak Curves

Figure 2 plots static (navy solid) and dynamic (red dashed) outbreak curves for each topology. In every case the dynamic model peaks **later** and **lower** than the static baseline:

| City type    | Static peak | Dynamic peak | Δ (days) | Static AR | Dynamic AR | ΔAR    |
| ------------ | ----------- | ------------ | -------- | --------- | ---------- | ------ |
| Dense        | Day 42.3    | Day 54.0     | **+11.7** | 72.3 %    | 58.0 %     | **−14.3 pp** |
| Polycentric  | Day 51.2    | Day 62.0     | **+10.8** | 63.8 %    | 52.0 %     | **−11.8 pp** |
| Sparse       | Day 68.7    | Day 76.0     | **+7.3**  | 48.5 %    | 42.0 %     | **−6.5 pp**  |

Paired t-test across the three cities: *t(2) = 4.82, p = 0.041*. Cohen's *d = 1.96* (large effect). The effect is monotonically larger in more densely-mixed cities, because faster infection growth in Dense networks triggers the behavioural-adaptation thresholds earlier and more often.

### C. Task 4 — Behavioural Scenario Analysis

Sweeping behavioural intensity on the Dense topology:

| Scenario        | Contact reduction | Peak day | Peak height | Attack rate | Δ vs no intervention |
| --------------- | ----------------- | -------- | ----------- | ----------- | -------------------- |
| No intervention | 0 %               | 42       | 18.4 %      | 72.3 %      | —                    |
| Mild            | 30 %              | 48       | 15.2 %      | 65.1 %      | −7.2 pp              |
| Moderate        | 50 %              | 54       | 12.8 %      | 58.0 %      | **−14.3 pp**         |
| Strong          | 70 %              | 60       | 10.5 %      | 52.3 %      | −20.0 pp             |

The marginal benefit of moving from Moderate (50 %) to Strong (70 %) is only **5.7 percentage points**, compared with **7.1 pp** for Mild → Moderate. Moderate intervention thus captures **~72 % of the benefit of Strong** at roughly half the social cost — a cost-effectiveness sweet spot. Figure 3 visualises this.

### D. Task 5 — Optimal Intervention Timing

Sweeping the **intervention activation day** while holding intensity at Moderate (50 %):

| City type    | Optimal intervention day | Maximum peak reduction |
| ------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------------- |
| Dense        | Day 18                   | 45 %                   |
| Polycentric  | Day 28                   | 35 %                   |
| Sparse       | Day 42                   | 38 %                   |

Acting **after** the optimal day produces a sharp drop in effectiveness; acting **before** wastes social capital. The 2× effectiveness claim is operationalised by comparing the topology-optimal timings against a single "universal" day-30 policy: the topology-aware policy achieves an average peak reduction of 39.3 %, vs. ~19 % for the universal policy.

### E. Task 6 — Validation Against St. Petersburg

Figure 5 (left) overlays the dynamic-model prediction (blue dashed) with the normalised St. Petersburg COVID-19 monitoring curve (black solid) for the Winter 2022 (Omicron) wave. The right panel is the predicted-vs-observed scatter.

| Metric        | Value | Interpretation                  |
| ------------- | ----- | ------------------------------- |
| **R²**        | 0.915 | Excellent out-of-sample fit     |
| **RMSE**      | 0.087 | Low prediction error            |
| **MAE**       | 0.065 | Very small absolute error       |
| **Pearson r** | 0.957 | Strong positive correlation     |

The **static baseline** achieves only R² = 0.62 on the same data, confirming that the **dynamic ingredients are necessary** for realistic forecasting of an Omicron-scale urban wave.

## VI. Discussion

### A. Why Dense Cities Are Most Affected by Dynamics

Across every metric (peak shift, attack-rate reduction, peak-height reduction) the Dense topology shows the largest gap between static and dynamic predictions. The mechanism is the **early triggering of behavioural thresholds**: Dense networks reach the 1 %, 5 %, and 10 % prevalence triggers earliest and most frequently, so the adaptive contact-reduction multiplier is engaged for the largest fraction of the simulation horizon. Sparse cities, by contrast, often never cross the 10 % threshold during the wave, so the strongest behavioural response is rarely activated.

### B. Diminishing Returns of Behavioural Intensity

The Mild → Moderate → Strong sweep (Section V-C) shows that the marginal benefit of contact-reduction intensity flattens past the 50 % mark. This is consistent with the **non-linear interaction** between contact rate and reproduction number: at very low contact rates, the effective R₀ is already well below 1 and further reduction yields little additional public-health benefit. In practical terms, **Moderate distancing is the cost-effective lever**, especially for Dense cities where the social cost of Strong measures is highest.

### C. Topology-Aware Intervention Strategy

Combining Sections V-D and V-E, the recommended strategies are:

| City type    | Recommended strategy                       | Timing window  | Expected peak reduction |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------ | -------------- | ----------------------- |
| Dense        | Target transportation hubs                 | Before day 20  | 40 – 50 %               |
| Polycentric  | Synchronised multi-centre response         | Before day 28  | 35 – 40 %               |
| Sparse       | Community-based containment                | Before day 42  | 35 – 40 %               |

These strategies are roughly **2× more effective** than a single uniform policy applied to all three topologies.

### D. Limitations

Four limitations should be noted.

1. **Network structure is fixed.** Only edge weights change over time; the topology itself does not rewire. Real-world social networks do restructure (job changes, school closures, friend-group turnover), which an adaptive-topology extension could capture.
2. **Behavioural parameters are not St. Petersburg-calibrated.** Thresholds and reduction multipliers are taken from published mobility studies. Local calibration with St. Petersburg mobility data would strengthen quantitative forecasts.
3. **Binary weekly pattern.** Holidays, school terms, and seasonal effects are not modelled.
4. **No age structure.** Real contact matrices are highly age-assortative; adding age stratification would change absolute attack rates although it is unlikely to change the qualitative comparison.

### E. Threats to Statistical Validity

The paired t-test in Section V-B is run on N = 3 cities, which is admittedly low-powered. The conclusion is corroborated by Cohen's *d* (1.96, a large effect) and ANOVA across the per-run replicates, so the headline finding is robust across statistical tests.

## VII. Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented the **first systematic comparison of static and dynamic SEIR predictions across three urban topology classes**, validated against real St. Petersburg COVID-19 data. Dynamic models systematically predict peak delays of 8–16 days and attack-rate reductions of 6–14 percentage points relative to static baselines, with Dense topologies most strongly affected. The dynamic model validates against real data with R² = 0.915 — well above the static baseline (R² = 0.62) — confirming that weekly rhythms and behavioural adaptation are necessary ingredients for realistic urban-wave forecasting.

Two practical implications follow. First, **public-health policy should be topology-aware**: Dense cities require intervention by day 18–20, Polycentric by day 28, Sparse by day 42. Second, **moderate (50 %) contact reduction is cost-effective**, capturing approximately 72 % of the benefit of stronger measures at roughly half the social cost.

**Future work** will pursue four directions:

1. **Local behavioural calibration** using St. Petersburg mobility data to refine the prevalence-threshold reduction multipliers.
2. **Age-structured contact mixing** to enable per-cohort severity and policy analysis.
3. **Real-time monitoring dashboard** integration with live case data and adaptive parameter updating.
4. **Reinforcement-learning policy discovery** — training an RL agent to choose intervention intensity and timing as a function of observed prevalence, hospital load, and mobility, potentially discovering policies that out-perform the fixed threshold rule across new variants and new cities.

The framework is released open-source at `https://github.com/yasiraabs123/urban-epidemic-research-final` to support reproducible follow-up work.
